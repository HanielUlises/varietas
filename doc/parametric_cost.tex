\title{\bfseries Measuring and removing the costs\\ of parametric Gr\"obner bases\\
for generated inverse kinematics}
\author{Haniel Ulises}
\date{30 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Inverse kinematics can be posed with the target adjoined to the coefficient field rather than
substituted into the polynomial ring, so that one Gr\"obner basis over $\QQ(\bp)$ answers every pose
and its action matrices can be emitted as source code. In the implementation studied here that
computation completed in milliseconds with two adjoined parameters and never with three, although a
tool position has three coordinates. We locate the cost, remove it, and locate and remove the cost
that appears behind it. Profiling attributes \SI{86}{\percent} of the time to the polynomial gcd used
to keep coefficients in lowest terms; repeating the computation over $\FF_p(\bp)$, and measuring the
subresultant gcd in isolation over both fields, shows that the cost grows as the $3.8$th power of the
operand size whatever the field, so that only a gcd built on evaluation and interpolation can help.
Brown's dense modular algorithm, certified by exact division over $\mathbb{Z}$ and returning the
cofactors that division computes, brings the exponent to $1.47$ and a gcd of $455$-term operands from
\SI{91}{\second} to \SI{4.6}{\milli\second}, and the three-parameter solve of the anthropomorphic arm,
which had not finished in \SI{900}{\second}, to about \SI{20}{\second}. On arms with offsets it still
does not finish in an hour, because the parameter polynomials carried through the completion swell to
$16\,000$ terms while the matrices finally produced have entries of fourteen. Reconstructing those
entries from fixed-pose solves over prime fields, by rational function interpolation, never forms the
intermediate expressions and solves every arm tested, three of which the usual decoupling of the base
joint cannot treat, in \SI{0.25}{\second} to \SI{47}{\second}; each result is checked exactly over
$\QQ$ at rational poses and agrees entry for entry with the symbolic solve where that finishes. Finally, a Newton step on the equations as posed
removes the residual tail of the emitted solvers, whose worst error falls from
\SI{6.5e-8}{\metre} to \SI{1.6e-15}{\metre} at a cost of \SI{0.6}{\micro\second} per call.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The inverse kinematics of a serial manipulator with revolute joints becomes a polynomial system under
the tangent half-angle substitution $t_i = \tan(q_i/2)$, and the resulting zero-dimensional ideal can
be solved by the eigenvalue method of M\"oller and Stetter~\cite{moller1995}. Classical treatments of
the general $6R$ arm reduce the problem to a univariate resultant of degree
sixteen~\cite{leeliang1988,raghavanroth1993,manochacanny1994,husty2007}, and code generators such as
IKFast~\cite{diankov2010} match an arm against a catalogue of solvable structures. The system studied
here takes a different route. The target pose is adjoined to the coefficient field, so that the
Gr\"obner basis is computed once for the manipulator and evaluated at each requested pose, and the
action matrices of the quotient algebra are emitted as source code. The number of solutions is then
the dimension of the quotient algebra, a certificate that no branch has been missed, and a call to the
emitted solver costs about as much as a few evaluations of the forward kinematics.

The obstacle is the cost of the offline computation. Writing $N$ for the number of actuated joints and
$P$ for the number of adjoined pose coordinates, only $P = N$ can give a finite, nonempty solution set
(Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}), and a tool position has $P \le 3$. The implementation completed
$P = N = 2$ in tens of milliseconds and had produced no result for $P = N = 3$ after
\SI{900}{\second}, which left every three-joint arm to a decoupling that applies only when the base
placement commutes with the base rotation. This paper follows the cost of the three-parameter
problem through two bottlenecks and removes each.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled measurement that separates the two usual explanations of a slow gcd over
  $\QQ(\bp)$ (Sections~\ref{sec:design}--\ref{sec:analysis}). Substituting $\FF_p$ for $\QQ$ does not
  make the problem finish, and the subresultant gcd costs the same power of the operand size over both
  fields, so that the coefficient field governs a bounded constant and computing the same remainder
  sequence modulo several primes cannot help.
\item An implementation of Brown's dense modular gcd~\cite{brown1971} in which the certifying division
  is carried out over $\mathbb{Z}$ and its quotients are returned as cofactors, with a correctness
  argument for the leading-monomial rule it relies on (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and a measurement of
  its effect in isolation and on the parametric solve, with ablations (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
\item The observation that, once the gcd is removed, the cost of the parametric solve on arms with
  offsets lies in intermediate expression swell, and a reconstruction of the action matrices from
  fixed-pose solves over prime fields~\cite{kaltofen1990,vonmanteuffel2015,peraro2016} that avoids it,
  checked exactly at rational poses (Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction}). Every three-joint arm tested,
  including three the decoupling cannot treat, one of them with three pairwise skew axes, is solved
  in under a minute.
\item A Newton refinement on the equations as posed, emitted into the generated solver, which removes
  the error the eigenvalue method incurs through ill-conditioned eigenvectors
  (Section~\ref{sec:refinement}).
\end{enumerate}

Sections~\ref{sec:design} to~\ref{sec:analysis} give the diagnosis in the order in which it was
carried out, beginning with the design of the field substitution and continuing with its results and
with what they imply for the choice of gcd. Section~\ref{sec:method} states the gcd adopted, Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction} the
reconstruction, Section~\ref{sec:refinement} the refinement, and Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}
measures all three. The implementation is part of the varietas library, available under the Apache
License~2.0 at \url{https://github.com/HanielUlises/varietas}, and the appendix lists the programs that
produce every figure reported.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

\subsection{Notation}

Let $k$ be a field and $k[\bt] = k[t_1,\dots,t_N]$ the polynomial ring in the joint variables. For a
monomial order $\prec$ and nonzero $f \in k[\bt]$, $\LM(f)$ denotes the leading monomial of $f$, and
for an ideal $I$ the initial ideal is
$\In(I) = \langle \LM(f) : f \in I \setminus \{0\} \rangle$. A finite $G \subset I$ is a Gr\"obner
basis when $\In(I) = \langle \LM(g) : g \in G\rangle$. The quotient $A = k[\bt]/I$ is a $k$-vector
space with basis the monomials outside $\In(I)$, called the standard monomials, and $I$ is
zero-dimensional exactly when that basis is finite, in which case $\dim_k A$ bounds the number of
points of $V(I)$ over $\bar{k}$ and equals it when $I$ is radical. We refer
to~\cite{coxlittleoshea} throughout for these facts and to~\cite{buchberger1965} for the completion
algorithm.

Saturation is written $I : h^\infty$. The half-angle substitution introduces denominators
$1 + t_i^2$, and clearing them attaches to $V(I)$ the loci $t_i = \pm\sqrt{-1}$, which correspond to
no configuration of the manipulator. Saturating by $h = \prod_i (1+t_i^2)$ removes them, so that
$\dim_k A$ counts configurations alone.

\subsection{The parametric formulation}

\begin{definition}[Parametric position problem]
\label{def:problem}
Let $\Phi : \Aff^N \to \Aff^3$ be the rational forward map of a manipulator with $N$ actuated joints,
written over the coefficient field $k$, with numerator components $\Phi_i$ and common denominator
$D$. Let $C \subseteq \{1,2,3\}$ with $|C| = P$ index the constrained tool coordinates, and adjoin
indeterminates $\bp = (p_1,\dots,p_P)$ to $k$. The parametric position ideal is
\[
  I \;=\; \Bigl\langle\, \Phi_{c_j}(\bt) - D(\bt)\,p_j \;:\; j = 1,\dots,P \,\Bigr\rangle : h^\infty
  \;\subseteq\; k(\bp)[\bt].
\]
\end{definition}

Coefficients are elements of $k(\bp)$, represented as a quotient of two polynomials in $\bp$ over $k$
and reduced to lowest terms after every arithmetic operation. Reduction requires a polynomial gcd in
$k[\bp]$, computed at the outset of this work by a subresultant remainder sequence in the sense of
Collins~\cite{collins1967}, and by the construction of Section~\ref{sec:method} at its end.
Without it the representations grow without bound under the repeated additions and multiplications of
the completion algorithm, so the reduction is a requirement of the formulation rather than an
economy.

\subsection{The admissible number of parameters}

The two results below restrict attention to $P = N$ and identify the three-parameter case as the
smallest spatial instance.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:under}
Let $I$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:problem} with $P < N$. Then every irreducible component of
$V(I)$ has dimension at least $N - P$, and $I$ fails to be zero-dimensional unless
$V(I) = \emptyset$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The ideal before saturation is generated by $P$ elements, so by Krull's height
theorem~\cite[Ch.~10]{eisenbud} every minimal prime over it has height at most $P$, whence every
component of its zero set has dimension at least $N - P$. Saturation deletes components and leaves
the dimension of those that survive unchanged, so the bound persists. The degenerate case
$V(I) = \emptyset$ arises when saturation deletes every component.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:over}
Let $I$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:problem} with $\bp$ algebraically independent over $k$, and
suppose $P > N$. If the Zariski closure of the image of $\Phi$ has dimension at most $N$, then
$I = \langle 1 \rangle$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $p_1,\dots,p_P$ satisfy no nontrivial polynomial relation over $k$, the point $\bp$ is a generic
point of $\Aff^P$. The attainable tool positions lie in a constructible set whose closure
$\overline{\operatorname{im}\Phi}$ has dimension at most $N < P$, hence is a proper closed subset of
$\Aff^P$ and is contained in the zero set of some nonzero $F \in k[\bp]$. Independence gives
$F(\bp) \neq 0$, so no $\bt$ satisfies the generators and the ideal is the unit ideal.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:pn}
A nonempty finite solution set requires $P = N$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:precondition}
The implementation uses Corollary~\ref{cor:pn} as a precondition test, rejecting $P \neq N$ by
counting before any completion is attempted. Under this restriction the three-parameter case is the
smallest one in which a manipulator positions a tool in space.
\end{remark}

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Substitution of the coefficient field}

The discriminating measurement replaces $\QQ$ by the prime field $\FF_p$ with $p = 2^{31} - 1$,
holding fixed the algorithm, the monomial order, the generators, the saturation and the number of
adjoined parameters. In $\FF_p$ an addition or multiplication is one machine-word operation followed
by a reduction, and coefficients do not grow, so any cost surviving the substitution is independent of
coefficient size. The substitution therefore separates the two accounts of Section~\ref{sec:introduction}
directly.

The field $\FF_p(\bp)$ is implemented as a quotient of two polynomials over $\FF_p$, normalised in the
same three steps used for $\QQ(\bp)$, which divide out the monomial content, divide out the polynomial
gcd, and scale the denominator to be monic. The remainder of the library is templated on the coefficient
type and is used without modification, so the two runs execute the same instruction sequence up to the
arithmetic itself.

\subsection{Validity control}

Reduction modulo a prime preserves the structure of a Gr\"obner basis for all but finitely many
primes. The exceptional primes are those for which the reduced basis acquires a different initial
ideal, and detecting them is the standard obstacle in modular Gr\"obner methods~\cite{arnold2003}. We
therefore record, for every completed run, the multiset of leading monomials of the reduced basis.

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:control}
Agreement of the initial ideals between the two fields is a necessary condition for the substitution
to be valid on a given input. Disagreement would indicate an unlucky prime and would invalidate a
comparison of running times on that input, the two runs having completed different ideals.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Systems}

Three systems are measured, each posed as in Definition~\ref{def:problem} with one adjoined coordinate
per joint, so that $P = N$ throughout in accordance with Corollary~\ref{cor:pn}. The first is a planar
two-link arm with two revolute joints about $z$ and unit links, constrained in $x$ and $y$, which has
two solutions. The second is the shoulder and elbow of an anthropomorphic arm, obtained by removing
its base joint in the manner of Pieper's decoupling~\cite{pieper1968} and constrained by a radius and
a height; it also has two solutions. The third is the anthropomorphic arm itself, a base yawing about
$z$ followed by two joints pitching about $y$, with unit links and all three coordinates constrained.
Completing that system over $\QQ$ at a fixed rational pose establishes independently that it has four
solutions. The first two systems act as controls in the sense of Remark~\ref{rem:control}.

\subsection{Instrumentation}

Three quantities are recorded beyond wall-clock time. The completion is timed separately from the
construction of the forward map and the residuals, since the latter also performs coefficient
arithmetic and would otherwise be conflated with the former. The main loop of the completion algorithm
reports the number of pairs processed, the current basis size, the pending pair count and the largest
basis polynomial. The normalisation of $k(\bp)$ reports the largest numerator or denominator it has
encountered, measured in terms.

\subsection{Environment}

Measurements were taken on an Intel Core i7-10870H at \SI{2.20}{\giga\hertz} running Linux 6.8,
compiled with g++ 11.4.0 at \texttt{-O2}, with GMP providing arbitrary-precision rational arithmetic.
In the gcd measurements of Section~\ref{sec:gcd}, points whose operands have total degree at most
eight are the median of five runs and larger points are single runs; the quantity of interest spans
five orders of magnitude and is insensitive to run-to-run variation at that scale.

\subsection{The three-parameter system}
\label{sec:three}

The anthropomorphic 3R system with all three coordinates constrained produced no result over
$\FF_p(x,y,z)$ within \SI{400}{\second}, nor over $\QQ(x,y,z)$ within \SI{900}{\second}. Construction
of the forward map and the residuals completed in under \SI{0.05}{\second} in both fields, so the
whole of the elapsed time is attributable to the completion.

Instrumenting the main loop localises the failure. Over $\FF_p(x,y,z)$ the run enters the loop with
four basis elements, six pending pairs and a largest basis polynomial of fourteen terms, and processes
one pair. No second pair was processed in the subsequent \SI{300}{\second}. The run is therefore
inside a single reduction, and the failure is not an accumulation of many basis elements.

Instrumenting the normalisation identifies what grows during that reduction. The largest parameter
polynomial appearing as a numerator or denominator reaches $460$ terms after \SI{93}{\second} and
$498$ terms after \SI{129}{\second}, with intervals of tens of seconds between consecutive
normalisations. Coefficients in $\FF_p$ do not grow, so these intervals measure the cost of a gcd in
$\FF_p[x,y,z]$ on operands of that size. This observation motivates the measurement of the next
section and fixes $m \approx 500$ as the operating point of interest.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Separation of the two effects}

The controls of Table~\ref{tab:controls} establish that the substitution of $\FF_p$ for $\QQ$
preserves the computation on the two-parameter inputs, by the criterion of Remark~\ref{rem:control}.
The three-parameter system then fails to terminate in a setting where coefficient arithmetic is a
machine-word operation and coefficients do not grow, which is inconsistent with the first of the two
accounts as an explanation of the failure.

Table~\ref{tab:gcd} quantifies what remains. The two fitted exponents agree to within $0.02$, so the
coefficient field affects the constant $\beta$ and leaves $\alpha$ unchanged, which appears in
Figure~\ref{fig:gcd} as two parallel lines. The exponent $\alpha \approx 3.8$ is a property of the
subresultant remainder sequence applied to multivariate operands. The sequence performs a number of
pseudo-divisions that grows with the degree in the main variable, each pseudo-division is a polynomial
multiplication and exact division over the remaining variables, and the content computations recurse
into those variables in turn~\cite{collins1967,brown1971}. The measured $\alpha$ is consistent with
that structure and is present over both fields.

\subsection{Consequences for modular gcd algorithms}

The term \emph{modular gcd} covers two constructions with different consequences here. The first
computes the same subresultant remainder sequence over several primes and reconstructs the result by
the Chinese remainder theorem, with a trial division to certify the candidate~\cite{brown1971}. It
addresses coefficient growth and leaves the sequence itself intact, so its benefit is bounded by the
ratio measured in Table~\ref{tab:gcd}, approximately a factor of $13$. Applied to a computation that
has not completed in \SI{900}{\second}, a factor of $13$ does not produce a usable result.

The second reduces the multivariate problem to univariate gcds at sampled points and recovers the
answer by interpolation, in the dense form due to Brown~\cite{brown1971} or the sparse form due to
Zippel~\cite{zippel1979}, with the Hensel-based variant of Moses and Yun~\cite{mosesyun1973} a further
alternative. Such a construction replaces the remainder sequence and therefore acts on $\alpha$ rather
than on $\beta$, which is what the measurement identifies as the governing quantity.

\subsection{The operating point}

Interpolating Table~\ref{tab:gcd} between $m = 455$ and $m = 680$ at the exponent $\alpha = 3.79$
places a single gcd at $m = 498$ at approximately \SI{9}{\second} over $\FF_p$ and, applying the
measured ratio, approximately \SI{125}{\second} over $\QQ$. Both figures agree with the intervals
between consecutive normalisations reported in Section~\ref{sec:three}. Since $m = 498$ lies inside the
range covered by Table~\ref{tab:gcd}, the estimate requires no extrapolation.

\section{An interpolating gcd}
\label{sec:method}

The construction adopted is Brown's dense modular algorithm~\cite{brown1971}, in the form given
in~\cite[Sec.~7.4]{geddes1992}, with two departures that the measurements of
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} show to matter. The certificate is an exact division over $\mathbb{Z}$
rather than over $\QQ$, and the quotients of that division are returned to the caller. This section
states the algorithm and the two facts about images on which its correctness rests.

Throughout, $A, B \in \QQ[\bp]$ are nonzero, $\prec$ is the monomial order under which the result is
to be monic, and $G = \gcd(A,B)$ is normalised to be monic under $\prec$. Clearing denominators and
dividing out the integer content writes $A = c_A A'$ with $c_A \in \QQ$ and $A' \in \mathbb{Z}[\bp]$
primitive, and likewise for $B$. By Gauss's lemma the primitive integer multiple $G'$ of $G$ divides
$A'$ and $B'$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\bp]$~\cite[Sec.~6.2]{vzgg2013}.

\subsection{Images and their leading monomials}

The algorithm maps the problem into simpler rings twice, first modulo a prime and then by specialising
a parameter, and computes a gcd there. What makes the approach work is that an image gcd can be too
large but never too small, and that being too large is visible in its leading monomial.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:prime-image}
Let $\ell$ be a prime not dividing $\operatorname{lc}_\prec(A')$, and let $H_\ell$ be the gcd of the
images $\bar A', \bar B'$ in $\FF_\ell[\bp]$. Then $\LM_\prec(H_\ell) \succeq \LM_\prec(G)$, with
equality if and only if $H_\ell$ is a nonzero scalar multiple of $\bar G'$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $G'$ divides $A'$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\bp]$ and leading coefficients are multiplicative,
$\operatorname{lc}_\prec(G')$ divides $\operatorname{lc}_\prec(A')$, so $\ell$ does not divide it and
$\LM_\prec(\bar G') = \LM_\prec(G)$. Reduction is a ring homomorphism, so $\bar G'$ divides $\bar A'$
and $\bar B'$ and hence $H_\ell = \bar G' Q$ for some $Q \in \FF_\ell[\bp]$. Leading monomials are
multiplicative over a field, so $\LM_\prec(H_\ell) = \LM_\prec(G)\,\LM_\prec(Q) \succeq \LM_\prec(G)$,
with equality exactly when $Q$ is a nonzero constant.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:point-image}
Let $A, B \in \FF_\ell[p_1,\dots,p_k]$ be primitive when read as polynomials in $p_1,\dots,p_{k-1}$
over $\FF_\ell[p_k]$, let $\operatorname{lc}$ denote the leading coefficient in that reading under
the lexicographic order, and let $\gamma = \gcd(\operatorname{lc} A, \operatorname{lc} B) \in
\FF_\ell[p_k]$. For $\alpha \in \FF_\ell$ with $\gamma(\alpha) \neq 0$, the gcd of $A|_{p_k=\alpha}$
and $B|_{p_k=\alpha}$ has leading monomial at least that of $G|_{p_k = \alpha}$, where $G = \gcd(A,B)$,
with equality if and only if it is a scalar multiple of $G|_{p_k=\alpha}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\operatorname{lc} G$ divides both $\operatorname{lc} A$ and $\operatorname{lc} B$, hence divides
$\gamma$, so it does not vanish at $\alpha$ and specialisation preserves the leading monomial of $G$.
The remainder of the argument is that of Proposition~\ref{prop:prime-image} with specialisation in
place of reduction.
\end{proof}

A prime or point whose image gcd has a leading monomial strictly larger than the true one is called
\emph{unlucky}. There are finitely many of each, since an unlucky prime divides a nonzero integer determined by
$A$ and $B$ and an unlucky point is a root of a nonzero polynomial in $p_k$, in both cases a
subresultant~\cite{brown1971}. Propositions~\ref{prop:prime-image} and~\ref{prop:point-image} turn this
into an operational rule, under which the images with the smallest leading monomial among those
computed are believed and any image with a larger one is discarded. An image with a smaller leading monomial than
the current one proves every image held so far unlucky, and the accumulated work is restarted from it.

\subsection{Recursion over a prime field}

Over $\FF_\ell$ the gcd of $A, B \in \FF_\ell[p_1,\dots,p_k]$ is computed recursively in $k$. The
contents of $A$ and $B$ as polynomials in $p_1,\dots,p_{k-1}$ over $\FF_\ell[p_k]$ are univariate gcds
and are removed first, their gcd being restored at the end. The remaining primitive parts are
specialised at points $\alpha$ with $\gamma(\alpha) \neq 0$, the gcd of the specialisations is computed
by the same procedure with $k-1$ variables, and each such image, which is monic, is scaled so that its
leading coefficient is $\gamma(\alpha)$. The scaling makes the images agree on a normalisation, namely
$\gamma G / \operatorname{lc}(G)$, whose degree in $p_k$ is at most
$\deg \gamma + \min(\deg_{p_k} A, \deg_{p_k} B)$; that many points plus one determine it, and it is
recovered coefficient by coefficient by Newton interpolation. At $k = 1$ the problem is univariate and
is a Euclidean algorithm on machine words.

The degree bound is a worst case, and the interpolant usually stops changing well before it is
reached. The implementation therefore tests a candidate as soon as a new point leaves the
interpolant unchanged, as well as once the bound is passed, and accepts the primitive part of the
interpolant only if it divides both operands in $\FF_\ell[p_1,\dots,p_k]$. A candidate that fails is
not an error but a signal to take more points. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures the effect of
this early termination.

Polynomials over $\FF_\ell$ are held as term lists sorted lexicographically with $p_1$ most
significant. At level $k$ the terms that agree in $p_1,\dots,p_{k-1}$ are then contiguous and ordered
by their exponent of $p_k$, so that reading a polynomial as one with coefficients in $\FF_\ell[p_k]$,
and specialising $p_k$, are single passes over the list with no sorting.

\subsection{Lifting to the rationals}

Primes are taken in decreasing order below $2^{31}$, so that a product of two residues fits a machine
word, and a prime dividing $\operatorname{lc}_\prec(A')$ or $\operatorname{lc}_\prec(B')$ is skipped as
Proposition~\ref{prop:prime-image} requires. Each image is made monic under $\prec$, which is the
normalisation of $G$, so that the images of lucky primes are the reductions of $G$ itself coefficient by
coefficient. Images sharing the smallest leading monomial are combined by the Chinese remainder theorem,
monomial by monomial over the union of their supports, and each combined coefficient is mapped back to a
rational number by rational reconstruction~\cite{wang1982,vzgg2013}.

A reconstruction is only a candidate. It is tested once two consecutive reconstructions agree, since the
test is the costly step and a reconstruction that is still changing is not worth testing, and the test
is exact, requiring the primitive integer form $G''$ of the candidate to divide $A'$ and $B'$ in
$\mathbb{Z}[\bp]$. By Gauss's lemma divisibility over $\QQ$ and over $\mathbb{Z}$ coincide for a
primitive divisor, so a quotient coefficient that fails to be an integer already decides the question.
The division keeps its remainder in an ordered map and subtracts each multiple of $G''$ in place, so
that a step costs the number of terms of the divisor rather than of the remainder, and it stops at the
first term that does not divide.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:correct}
The procedure returns $\gcd(A,B)$, monic under $\prec$, and terminates.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every image combined into a candidate $C$ has the same leading monomial $L$ and coefficient one there,
and none has a term above $L$, so $\LM(C) = L$, and $L \succeq \LM(G)$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:prime-image}. If $C$ is returned it divides $A$ and $B$, hence divides $G$, so
$L \preceq \LM(G)$. Therefore $\LM(C) = \LM(G)$, the quotient $G/C$ is a constant, and it is one because
both are monic. For termination, all but finitely many primes
are lucky, the images of lucky primes are the reductions of $G$, and rational reconstruction recovers
every coefficient of $G$ once the product of the primes exceeds twice the square of the largest
numerator or denominator among them~\cite[Sec.~5.10]{vzgg2013}. From then on consecutive
reconstructions agree and equal $G$, and the test succeeds.
\end{proof}

Two consequences are used by the caller. If the image modulo any single admissible prime is constant,
Proposition~\ref{prop:prime-image} shows that $G$ is constant, and the procedure returns after one prime
without a trial division; most calls made by normalisation end this way. And the division that certifies
$G$ has $A'/G''$ and $B'/G''$ as its quotients, from which the cofactors $A/G$ and $B/G$ follow by a
rational scaling. Normalisation needs exactly these cofactors, so the procedure returns them with $G$,
and no second division is performed.

The implementation formerly placed a heuristic in front of the gcd, which specialised every
parameter but one at fixed values, examined the resulting univariate gcds for a common factor, and
skipped the exact gcd when none appeared. That test gave evidence of coprimality rather than proof,
which was admissible only because a skipped cancellation costs size and never correctness. The first
consequence above supplies proof at a comparable price, and the heuristic is removed, so that every
coefficient is now held in lowest terms.

\section{Reconstruction from fixed poses}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

With the gcd replaced the three-parameter problem finishes on the anthropomorphic arm, and
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} shows that on arms with offsets it still does not. The parameter
polynomials carried through the completion then reach $16\,000$ terms, the gcd accounts for a quarter
of the time rather than most of it, and the rest is arithmetic on those polynomials. The action
matrices the computation would produce on that arm have entries of at most fourteen terms. The
intermediate expressions are the cost, and none of them is needed for the answer.

The action matrices $M_1,\dots,M_N$ and the normal forms of the variables, whose entries are the
rational functions of the pose that the emitted solver evaluates, are therefore recovered without a
Gr\"obner basis over $\QQ(\bp)$. The approach is black-box rational function
reconstruction~\cite{kaltofen1990}, of the kind that has made finite-field methods standard in the
reduction of Feynman integrals~\cite{vonmanteuffel2015,peraro2016}.

\subsection{The black box}

Fix a prime $\ell$ and a pose $\pi \in \FF_\ell^P$. The kinematic ideal at $\pi$ is generated by
$\Phi_{c_j}(\bt) - D(\bt)\,\pi_j$ and saturated by $\prod_i(1+t_i^2)$ exactly as in
Definition~\ref{def:problem}, but its coefficients are elements of $\FF_\ell$ and it has no
parameters. Its reduced Gr\"obner basis, standard monomials, action matrices and normal forms are
computed by the same generic code instantiated over $\FF_\ell$, and returned as a vector of
$N d^2 + N d$ field elements, where $d = \dim A$.

The standard monomials at a general pose are determined once, from three rational poses solved over
$\QQ$, as the set on which two of them agree. A sample whose standard monomials differ is on a
special fibre and is discarded. For $\pi$ outside a proper Zariski-closed set, the reduced basis at
$\pi$ is the specialisation of the reduced basis over $\QQ(\bp)$, and so are the matrices, entry by
entry; this is the property that makes the samples values of the functions sought.

\subsection{Reconstruction modulo a prime}

Each entry is a rational function $f = n/m$ of the pose, in lowest terms. Its degrees are found first,
on a random line $\pi(s) = u + s w$, along which $f$ is a univariate rational function whose
numerator and denominator degrees are, for a line in general position, the total degrees of $n$ and
$m$. They are read off by interpolating the samples along the line and running the extended Euclidean
algorithm against the product of the $(s - s_i)$, choosing the step with the quotient of largest
degree~\cite{monagan2004}, and the number of samples on the line is increased until every entry is
reconstructed with at least two samples to spare.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:line}
Let $f = n/m$ in lowest terms with $\deg n = a$ and $\deg m = b$. The pairs $(n', m')$ of polynomials
with $\deg n' \le a$, $\deg m' \le b$ and $n' m = n m'$ form a one-dimensional space.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
From $n' m = n m'$ and $\gcd(n,m) = 1$, $m$ divides $m'$; by degree $m' = c\,m$ for a constant $c$,
and then $n' = c\,n$.
\end{proof}

With the degrees known, each sample $(\pi_r, v_r)$ gives one linear equation
\[
  \sum_{|\alpha| \le a} n'_\alpha\, \pi_r^\alpha \;-\; v_r \sum_{|\beta| \le b} m'_\beta\, \pi_r^\beta \;=\; 0
\]
in the coefficients of $n'$ and $m'$ over all monomials of total degree at most $a$ and $b$. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:line} the solution space of the full system is a line, and a system built from
enough random samples has the same solution space except on a proper algebraic subset of the choices
of samples. The kernel is found by Gaussian elimination over $\FF_\ell$ and normalised so that the
coefficient of the leading monomial of $m'$ under the graded reverse lexicographic order is one. That
is how $f$ is normalised over $\QQ$, so the normalised vector modulo every prime that preserves this
leading monomial is the reduction of the same rational vector.

The samples on the line are not used in these systems, since points on one line do not determine a
polynomial in several variables, and a system filled with them has a kernel larger than a line. After
the first prime the support of each entry is known, and later primes solve only for the monomials the
first prime found, which on a sparse answer shrinks both the systems and the number of samples they
need. A term that the first prime missed by vanishing modulo it makes the smaller system inconsistent,
and the dense systems are then used again.

\subsection{Lifting and checking}

The normalised coefficients modulo successive primes are combined by the Chinese remainder theorem and
mapped to rationals by rational reconstruction, as in Section~\ref{sec:method}, until two consecutive
primes leave every reconstruction unchanged. Unlike the modular gcd, the result cannot be certified
by a division, because the entries exist only as values. It is instead checked exactly. At two of the
rational poses already solved over $\QQ$, or more if the caller asks, the reconstructed entries are
evaluated and compared with the entries of the matrices computed there, and any disagreement, or a
pole of the reconstruction where $\QQ$ has a value, is reported as a failure rather than returned.

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:probability}
The check poses have coordinates drawn from the $2^{21}+1$ dyadic rationals $k/1024$ with
$|k| \le 2^{20}$. A wrong entry $\tilde f = \tilde n/\tilde m \neq f = n/m$, reconstructed under the
same degree bounds $(a, b)$, agrees with $f$ at a pose only if the pose is a zero of
$\tilde n m - n \tilde m$, a nonzero polynomial of total degree at most $a + b$, which is $16$ on the
arms of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. If the check poses are regarded as drawn uniformly and
independently of the reconstruction, the Schwartz--Zippel lemma~\cite{schwartz1980,zippel1979} bounds
the probability of that by $16/(2^{21}+1) < 7.7 \times 10^{-6}$ per pose, and the probability that an
error survives two poses by $6 \times 10^{-11}$. The implementation fixes the seed, so that a
generated header does not depend on when it was generated; the bound describes the choice of seed,
and the check does not replace a proof (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).
\end{remark}

\section{Refining the emitted solver}
\label{sec:refinement}

The emitted solver forms a separating combination $\sum_i c_i M_i$ of the action matrices at the
requested pose, computes the eigenvectors of its transpose, which are the evaluation functionals at the
solutions, and reads each solution off an eigenvector through the normal forms of the variables. The
eigenvectors are computed to backward stability only. Near a pose at which two solutions give the
separating form nearly the same value, the eigenvectors, and the solutions read from them, lose
accuracy that the matrices themselves did not, and an emitted solver whose matrices are exact to
rounding returns points with residuals many orders of magnitude above it.

Both routes have at hand the equations as posed, the polynomials
$\Phi_{c_k}(\bt) - D(\bt)\,p_k$ in $\bt$ over $\QQ$, in which the pose enters linearly. They
are emitted into the solver with their Jacobian, differentiated exactly, and each solution returned by
the eigenvalue method is refined by at most two Newton steps, a step being kept only if it lowers the
norm of the residual. A solution already at rounding level is returned unchanged, and near a singular
configuration, where the Jacobian is unreliable, a step that would increase the residual is not taken.
The refinement changes nothing about the count of solutions, which remains $\dim A$.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Non-termination is observed, not proved.} The statements that a computation does not
finish are bounded observations, of \SI{400}{\second} and \SI{900}{\second} for the subresultant runs of
Section~\ref{sec:results}, and one hour for the symbolic runs on arms with offsets in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, except the one with the displaced elbow, which the system terminated
after 54 minutes with 29\,GB of memory in use, a first attempt having been killed earlier. The instrumentation that shows where those runs spend their time, a
single reduction in the first case and operands growing past $16\,000$ terms in the second, is the
stronger evidence.

\paragraph{One prime for the substitution.} The control of Remark~\ref{rem:control} verifies the prime
used for the field substitution only on the two-parameter inputs; on the three-parameter input no
verification is available. An unlucky prime would be expected to alter the initial ideal and the
course of the computation, not to turn a terminating computation into a non-terminating one.

\paragraph{Dense operands.} The operands of Table~\ref{tab:gcd} are dense of their total degree,
whereas the parameter polynomials of the pipeline need not be. Density makes the number of terms an
unambiguous size measure, at the cost of favouring the dense interpolation adopted over the sparse
method of~\cite{zippel1979} in any comparison between the two, which was not made.

\paragraph{The reconstruction is checked, not certified.} The modular gcd ends in an exact division
and is always correct; the reconstruction of Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction} has no such test, and
its exact checks at rational poses bound the probability of an undetected error
(Remark~\ref{rem:probability}) without excluding it. The symbolic solve therefore remains the default
of the implementation, and the reconstruction an option. On the two three-joint arms and the two
two-joint problems where both finish, the two agree in every entry. A certificate, such as an a priori degree bound that would turn finitely many
exact checks into a proof, is not provided.

\paragraph{Scope of the arms.} Five three-joint arms were measured. They cover the shapes that decide
the cost in this setting, links with and without offsets, a base that does and does not admit the
decoupling, and pairwise skew axes, but they are not a sample of industrial manipulators, whose
positioning subsystems are mostly of the first two kinds. Arms with prismatic joints were not
measured.

\paragraph{One completion algorithm.} All Gr\"obner bases were computed with one implementation of
Buchberger's algorithm with the normal selection strategy under the graded reverse lexicographic
order. A completion of the F4 family~\cite{faugere1999} would change the number and shape of the
coefficient operations in the symbolic solve, and the cost of each sample in the reconstruction,
where most of its time is spent; neither was measured.

\paragraph{The half-angle chart.} The emitted full solvers inherit a pole of the half-angle
substitution. A target reachable only with a joint at exactly $\pi$ in one family of solutions has a
quotient of smaller dimension, and on the arms with a base yawing about $z$ such targets fill the whole
plane $y = 0$, where the solver reports the pose as outside its chart. The decoupled solvers recover the
base angle by an arctangent and are not affected.

\paragraph{One machine.} All timings were taken on one machine, with the compiler and flags of
Section~\ref{sec:design}. The ratios between methods, which are what the conclusions rest on, span
one to four orders of magnitude and are not sensitive to the machine at that scale.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The three-parameter parametric position problem did not finish over $\FF_p(x,y,z)$, so its cost was
not in the arithmetic on rational coefficients. The subresultant gcd costs about the $3.8$th power of
the operand size over both fields, the field contributing a factor of about $13$, so that a modular
gcd formed by running the same remainder sequence over several primes could not have helped, and a
gcd built on evaluation and interpolation could. Brown's dense algorithm, certified by exact division
over $\mathbb{Z}$ and returning its cofactors, brings the exponent to $1.5$ and the three-parameter
solve of the anthropomorphic arm from no result in \SI{900}{\second} to about \SI{20}{\second}.

That exposes the next cost rather than ending the problem. On arms with offsets the symbolic solve
still does not finish in an hour, and the reason is no longer the gcd but the size of the
intermediate rational functions, which exceed the final ones by three orders of magnitude.
Reconstructing the final ones from fixed-pose solves over prime fields avoids them entirely, and
every three-joint arm measured is then solved in under a minute, most in under a second, including three the
decoupling cannot treat, one with three pairwise skew axes, and every result agrees exactly
with an independent computation over $\QQ$ at rational poses and configurations.

The emitted solvers are fast and, with a Newton step on the equations as posed, accurate to
rounding. A decoupled solve costs \SI{1.5}{\micro\second} and its worst residual over $71\,658$
configurations is \SI{1.6e-15}{\metre}, while a full three-joint solve costs \SI{6}{\micro\second}.
Where an arm admits the decoupling it remains the better route, exact, fastest to produce and to call,
and free of the half-angle pole; where it does not, the full solve now exists. Certifying the
reconstruction, a second chart for the half-angle substitution, and a faster fixed-pose completion
over prime fields are the work that remains.

\section{Reproduction}
\label{app:repro}

From the repository root, with the workspace built by \texttt{colcon build}:

\begin{verbatim}
CXX="g++ -std=c++17 -O2"
INC="-Ivarietas_core/include -Ivarietas_codegen/include \
     -Ivarietas_kinematics/include -Ivarietas_ik/include \
     -Idoc/experiments -I/usr/include/eigen3"

$CXX doc/experiments/field_cost.cpp -o field_cost $INC -lgmpxx -lgmp
./field_cost planar      # two-parameter control, both fields
./field_cost reduced     # two-parameter control, both fields
./field_cost spatial-fp  # three parameters over F_p(x,y,z); does not terminate
./field_cost spatial-q   # three parameters over Q(x,y,z); Table 4

$CXX doc/experiments/gcd_cost.cpp -o gcd_cost $INC -lgmpxx -lgmp
./gcd_cost subresultant  # Table 2 and the fitted exponents
./gcd_cost interpolating # the modular gcd on the same operands

$CXX doc/experiments/spatial_cost.cpp -o spatial_cost $INC -lgmpxx -lgmp
./spatial_cost           # Table 5, reconstructed, with the exact checks
./spatial_cost --symbolic anthropomorphic   # and the symbolic solve beside it

$CXX doc/experiments/solver_cost.cpp -o solver_cost $INC \
    -Ibuild/varietas_demo/generated -lgmpxx -lgmp
./solver_cost            # the decoupled solver at run time, and the baseline

ros2 run varietas_urdf urdf_codegen \
    varietas_demo/urdf/anthropomorphic_offset_3r.urdf full_offset_3r.hpp \
    --name full_offset_3r_ik --reconstruct
$CXX doc/experiments/full_solver_cost.cpp -o full_solver_cost \
    -I. -Ibuild/varietas_demo/generated -I/usr/include/eigen3
./full_solver_cost       # the full and decoupled solvers side by side
\end{verbatim}

\noindent
Adding \verb|-DVARIETAS_EXPERIMENT_TRACE| to the first build reports the largest parameter polynomial
encountered during normalisation over $\FF_p(\bp)$, the source of the figure $m = 498$. The pair-queue
statistics of Section~\ref{sec:three} were obtained with a temporarily instrumented copy of the
completion loop, and the ablations of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} with copies of the headers of the
modular gcd and of the rational function field, modified as the table describes. The
modular gcd keeps its own count of calls, primes and time, which \texttt{field\_cost} prints.
